Drum manufacturing rules
Cable-capacity formula
 
Stowage & weight

Packing factor k derates the geometric capacity for cross-overs and uneven traverse — the two conventions in circulation differ by π/4, so pick the one your formula is written in and the planner converts. Manufacturing size step is the increment every flange, barrel and clear width is rounded up to. Total axial protrusion is spindle stubs, bolt heads or nailing strips standing proud of the flanges — it adds to the overall width the container has to swallow, but not to the winding space. Spare length sizes each drum for a little more than the put-up so production overlength still goes on.

The maths behind it
Barrel diameter from cable OD
B = max( OD × ratio , minimum barrel Ø ) rounded up to the size step

Check this against your cable spec. The ratio here is a diameter multiple. If your spec quotes a minimum bending radius of 15 × OD, the equivalent barrel diameter is 30 × OD — enter 30, not 15. Every drum card shows the bend radius the chosen barrel actually delivers so you can check it the other way round.

Length that fits on a drum
Fe = F − 2 × freeboard c π/4 form Direct form L = π · k · W · ( Fe² − B² ) L = k · W · ( Fe² − B² ) ───────────────────────── ───────────────────── 4 · d² · 1000 d² · 1000

The same equation written two ways — the π/4 either sits in the formula or gets folded into k, so k ≈ 0.90 in the left form is k ≈ 0.70 in the right one. Feed either into the settings and say which it is; the planner converts and shows you both. Getting this wrong is a 22% error in capacity, in the unsafe direction.

The left form is the exact turn-by-turn count — W/d turns per layer over (Fe−B)/2d layers at a mean diameter of (Fe+B)/2 — with k derating for cross-overs. It treats layers and turns as continuous; the whole-layer and turns-per-layer counts on each drum card show you how much of the last layer is real.

How the container is packed

The container starts as one empty box. The packer repeatedly picks the block of drums that buys the most drums for the least extra container length, drops it into a corner of a free box, and guillotine-splits what is left into up to three smaller free boxes. Those leftovers — the lane beside a wide drum, the headroom above a short stack, the pocket at the door end — go back in the pool, so a later cable can fill space an earlier one paid for.

Each candidate block is tried in every allowed orientation, square-gridded and interlocked, and the drum's own flange diameter is a free variable swept across every manufacturable size. In each orientation the drum shows a circular face in one plane; that is the plane the packer tiles:

Upright circle on the floor (length × width), stacks up the height Sideways circle on the side (length × height), runs back across the width Flange fwd circle in the cross-section (width × height), repeats along the length

Interlocking offsets alternate rows by half a flange so they drop into the valleys between their neighbours, cutting the row pitch from F to 0.866 F. It only wins when the span has enough slack for the offset row to keep its full count, so the packer tries both and keeps whichever holds more — it never assumes the nested stack is better. Interlocked stacks need chocks or wedges; switch the option off if your stow plan forbids them.

  • Upright — standing on a flange, axis vertical. A circle in plan.
  • Sideways — axis across the box, the way a drum rolls in through the doors. A circle in elevation.
  • Flange forward — axis fore-and-aft, flange facing the doors. A rectangle in both views. Wins for big-flange, narrow drums, and it is the only orientation whose cross-section packs in two directions at once.
Empty drum mass (estimate)
tare = [ 2 · (π/4) · F² · flange thk + π · B · W · barrel wall ] × density × batten allowance

A timber-drum approximation for the payload check. Adjust the density and batten allowance to match your drums. The same timber volume is what the planner minimises when it has slack to spend, so "smaller drum" means less material, not just a smaller envelope.

Container dimensions are ISO nominal internals; verify against the actual box before booking. The packer optimises for length used, not for fore-and-aft weight distribution. Lashing, dunnage, cradles, chocks and axle-weight rules are not modelled.
